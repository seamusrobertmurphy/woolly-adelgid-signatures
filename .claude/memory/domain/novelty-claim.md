# The novelty claim and what has already refuted weaker versions

2026-08-14: The claim is that no study attributes forest damage between two insect agents
attacking the **same host species**. Three weaker formulations have already been refuted,
so do not restate the claim without the same-host qualifier. Matters because each
refutation cost a rewrite of the introduction.

Refuted: "no study attributes between insect agents" is false. Senf, Pflugmacher, Wulder
and Hostert 2015 (doi 10.1016/j.rse.2015.09.019) separated mountain pine beetle from
western spruce budworm in British Columbia with LandTrendr and random forests, at 75.3 to
88.0 percent depending on whether stands were pure or mixed host. Their hosts differ,
which is the only reason the same-host claim survives.

Refuted: "no study uses lidar structure to differentiate agents" is false. Atkins et al.
2020 (doi 10.1002/ecs2.3156) used terrestrial lidar structural traits across six agents
including hemlock woolly adelgid.

2026-08-14: Senf et al.'s mixed-host result is the study's best motivation and should be
used as such: "in the case of mixed host stands, nearly 45% of the western spruce budworm
disturbances were falsely attributed to mountain pine beetle." Host composition does much
of the work that looks like damage discrimination. Matters because it turns a gap
assertion into empirical support.

2026-08-14: The structural evidence is mixed and both sides concern the congener.
Alveshere et al. 2025 (doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2025.122698) report hemlock woolly adelgid
influences canopy structural complexity; Choi, LaRue and Atkins 2023
(doi 10.1111/1365-2745.14145) found the impact could not be clearly detected, attributing
it to compensatory growth. Alveshere is cited from title and publisher record only:
Elsevier, Springer and SSRN all return 403, so no quantitative result is quoted from it
and it must be read before it carries weight.
